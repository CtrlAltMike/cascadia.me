After an event

Organize the people who are here.

Once the immediate danger has passed, make a shared picture of what people can see, what they need, and what they can safely offer. Keep the method simple enough to survive a dead battery.

Use the eight-step method Open the printable field packet Choose an event insert

Personal safety and current official instructions come first. Don't enter damaged buildings, cross floodwater, approach downed lines, move hazardous materials, or take on medical, fire, utility, or rescue work beyond your training.

The method

Eight steps. Then repeat what still matters.

Temporary structure helps people work together. It shouldn't make anyone the permanent boss of the block.

  1. Gather safely and follow current instructions.

    Use a known outdoor meeting place when it is safe. Check the current evacuation, shelter, road, utility, health, and weather information for your location.

  2. Choose temporary roles.

    Ask for one coordinator, one recorder, and one person to watch official information. Name backups. Roles can change as people tire or leave.

  3. Build a common picture.

    Record who is accounted for, urgent needs, visible damage, blocked routes, working services, and useful offers. Add a time and source to every report.

  4. Set the first priorities.

    Start with life safety, changing hazards, people who cannot wait, and problems that will get worse quickly. Everything else can join the list without jumping the line.

  5. Match needs with offers.

    Pair a specific need with a willing person, a safe method, and the right equipment. Don't assume someone can help because of their job, age, body, vehicle, or supplies.

  6. Assign work with a return time.

    Write the task, who accepted it, where they are going, and when to check back. Avoid sending anyone alone when conditions or the work make that unsafe.

  7. Hand useful facts to the responsible authority.

    Report the location, observable condition, number of people affected, urgent access issue, action already taken, and time observed. Say clearly what is confirmed and what is secondhand.

  8. Recheck and stand people down.

    Review priorities on a visible schedule. Close completed tasks, correct old information, rest people, and stop local activity when officials take over or the work is no longer safe or useful.

First questions

Ask for decisions, not stories.

Keep questions short, respectful, and tied to what someone needs or can safely do now.

Life safety

  • Is anyone in immediate danger?
  • Does anyone need urgent medical help or a welfare check?
  • Is there a changing fire, water, gas, electrical, structural, air, or access hazard?

Continuity

  • Who can't safely wait for water, medication, power, temperature control, mobility help, or communication?
  • What ordinary function is failing now?
  • What will fail next if nothing changes?

Capacity

  • Who is available and willing?
  • What training, language, equipment, space, transport, or local knowledge is offered?
  • What limits the offer?

Information

  • What was directly observed, where, and when?
  • What was reported by someone else?
  • Which public authority needs the information, and has it been handed off?

A useful handoff

Make it easy for the next person to act.

A short, time-stamped report is more useful than a confident guess.

Keep private details private

A public board may show a unit or address, general status, time checked, and whether help is requested. Keep medical details, phone numbers, access codes, and other sensitive information on a controlled sheet—and only when they're needed.

Reviewed July 25, 2026. This field method supports informal neighborhood coordination; it doesn't replace local emergency plans, trained responders, incident command, or current official instructions.
