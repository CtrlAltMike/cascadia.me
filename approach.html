Why this exists

Preparedness is an act of care.

I made Cascadia.me for people who live here and want to be more ready without making fear the center of the story. It should help you understand this place, make a plan that fits your household, and know where to turn when conditions change.

Written and maintained in Washington State.

I began with my own family.

In 2016, while raising a young family in Washington, I read Kathryn Schulz’s Pulitzer Prize–winning article about the Cascadia Subduction Zone. I kept returning to one question: if a major earthquake happened, what would the people I love need me to have done already?

First I built a family-sized emergency kit. Then I kept reading. I wanted to understand how this region’s geology and weather meet the roads, power, water, medicine, animals, and people that make up an actual household.

Most of what I found was either official information with no sense of how the pieces fit together, or survivalist advice that made fear a way of life. I wanted calm, specific help that respected both the science and the person reading it.

I am not a seismologist, fire scientist, meteorologist, or emergency manager. I live in Washington State, and I write and maintain this site. I follow professional guidance, link to original sources, and correct the work when a source changes or an expert, event, or reader shows me that something is incomplete.

Cascadia.me does not replace local emergency management. It helps you understand the regional picture and make early choices at home. When conditions change, the authority responsible for your location has the final word.

I show my work.

A map, alert, or forecast should not ask for blind trust. Before I put it here, I ask three questions. You can use the same questions when you read it.

  1. 01

    What are you looking at?

    I name the thing itself—a gauge reading, station report, forecast, fire perimeter, road update, alert, or agency notice—along with who published it, where it applies, and when it was updated. Every guide shows when I last reviewed it. Live information shows the newest source time available; every feed follows its own clock and covers its own area.

  2. 02

    What can it honestly tell you?

    A forecast describes a possible future, not what is happening now. Forecast wind smooths mountain terrain; a weather station reports one point. A fire perimeter shows where a boundary was mapped, not where the fire will spread next. A planning map can show exposure; it cannot tell you whether one property is safe.

    If a feed fails, is partial, or a map is blank, I do not turn that into an all-clear. It may mean no report, delayed reporting, incomplete coverage, a source outage, or a layer that does not include that place.

  3. 03

    What can you decide early?

    Preparation gives you choices to make early: where to meet, when you would leave, who may need help, and what your household cannot go without. During an event, follow current instructions from the authority responsible for your location.

    Cascadia crosses many lines on a map, and warning systems do not all use the same words. The authority may be a Tribe or First Nation, a city or regional government, a weather or fire service, a road agency, or a utility. I use the issuing authority’s wording and link you back to it. A summary here never outranks an evacuation order, closure, warning, or utility notice.

    Cascadia.me cannot see the road outside your home, the smoke in your valley, or your household’s needs. Use what you know about your surroundings alongside official direction.

Not every map is saying the same thing.

I use four labels so you can tell the difference at a glance.

Observed
A recent reading or report from a gauge, station, incident system, road source, or agency. It tells you what that source recorded at a particular place and time—no more.
Forecast
A model’s estimate for a future time. It can be useful without being exact, especially around ridges, valleys, shorelines, and individual streets.
Historical or planning
Past records or a broad view of exposure. These layers can help you ask better questions; they do not predict the next event or decide whether one property is safe.
Official instruction
A warning, order, closure, or safety instruction from the authority responsible for the place and event. When conditions are changing, this is the one to follow.

Where I look first.

I start as close to the original information as I can: the agency that issued an alert, the scientist or public-health authority that published the guidance, the Tribe, First Nation, or Nation speaking for itself, or the road or utility operator responsible for the update. These are some of the sources I return to:

  • U.S. Geological Survey
  • National Weather Service
  • FEMA
  • Washington Emergency Management
  • Oregon Emergency Management
  • EmergencyInfoBC
  • PreparedBC
  • BC Wildfire Service
  • Environment and Climate Change Canada

Coverage follows the source. Northern California appears in the wider regional map and earthquake context, but it is not part of the current-condition feed. Every guide and live layer names the places it actually covers.

Here’s what I promise.

I will not make a hazard louder to sell you something.

When I combine recurring household details into one example, I call it a composite. The medicine that must stay cold, the animal that cannot be left behind, the ferry that may stop, the neighbor who needs a ride, and the apartment with little storage all make preparedness real. I will not pass a composite off as one survivor’s testimony or as something I lived myself.

Cascadia is connected geography, not one culture. I do not use Indigenous art as regional decoration or present Indigenous knowledge as mine. When Indigenous knowledge, cultural burning, or guidance from a specific Tribe, First Nation, or Nation belongs here, I name the Nation, organization, and source it came from.

The work stays independent. Cascadia.me has no advertising or affiliate links, and a recommendation never earns me a commission. Safety guidance is checked against linked official and scientific sources, but Cascadia.me is not endorsed by those agencies. Reader support helps maintain the work; the guides, household workbook, and Atlas will remain public, and nothing moves behind a paywall.

If something here is wrong or incomplete, I want to know. The point is not to sound certain. It is to help you feel less overwhelmed, see the next useful choice, and care for the people and place that are yours.
