An author’s note

Preparedness is an act of care.

I built Cascadia.me because I wanted preparedness writing that treated people like adults: honest about risk, specific to this region, genuinely useful, and warm enough to keep reading.

Why this exists

A knowledgeable neighbor, not another brochure.

You prepare because you have people to protect, a place you love, and a life you want to keep. Every emergency kit is an act of imagination: a story about a future that has not happened yet, told by someone who decided to take it seriously.

That impulse began for me in 2016, when I read an article about the Cascadia Subduction Zone while raising a young family in Washington. The thought of being helpless after an earthquake unsettled me enough to build a family-sized emergency kit—not because I was an expert, but because I was a parent who wanted a better answer than fear.

The hard part was not finding another checklist. It was connecting regional geology, weather, infrastructure, official language, household needs, and neighbor-to-neighbor care. Most preparedness resources seemed to be either frightening or bloodless. I wanted something that sounded like it came from someone who lived here and respected both the reader and the place.

I am not a seismologist, fire scientist, meteorologist, or emergency manager. I research and maintain this site, link readers to professional guidance, and correct the work when a source, expert, event, or reader shows that something is incomplete. Cascadia.me does not replace local emergency management. It helps a person understand the regional story well enough to hear local instructions in context.

The evidence method

Trust what you can trace.

Preparedness information is useful only when you can tell what was reported, what was interpreted, what remains uncertain, and who is responsible for the instruction.

01 / Observe

What the source reports

A gauge reading, alert, perimeter, forecast, road status, or agency notice—along with its source and update time when available.

02 / Interpret

What the evidence supports

A forecast is not an observation; a fire perimeter is not a spread prediction; a planning layer is not a parcel determination.

03 / Decide

Who gives the instruction

Your local authority, emergency manager, weather service, fire agency, road agency, or utility remains the source for current orders and protective action.

How I work

Sources are part of the sentence.

Primary and traceable sources

The guides begin with government, scientific, public-health, emergency-management, Tribal or Nation-specific, and other responsible primary sources. A reviewed date belongs on the page. Live information carries the newest source time available, and each connected source keeps its own cadence and geographic limits.

A failed or partial feed never becomes an all-clear. If a map is blank, that can mean no report, delayed reporting, incomplete geography, a source outage, or a layer that does not cover that jurisdiction.

Blank does not mean all clear

Regional views are valuable because they reveal connections, but they can also look more exact than the evidence deserves. Forecast wind smooths mountain terrain. Station wind describes one reported point. Fire perimeters can lag changing conditions. Flood-planning layers do not establish what is safe on a particular parcel.

Limits belong beside the information they qualify. The goal is not to bury the reader in caveats; it is to keep a useful picture from pretending to be a guarantee.

The instruction stays local

Cascadia crosses international, state, provincial, county, municipal, Tribal, and First Nations jurisdictions. The site preserves the vocabulary used by the responsible authority rather than forcing unlike warning systems into one regional label.

During an event, the current instruction comes from the local authority responsible for your location. Cascadia.me helps with orientation and preparation; it does not overrule an evacuation order, road closure, weather warning, utility notice, or local knowledge.

The reader relationship

Human without pretending to have lived every story.

Recognizable households make preparedness legible: the medicine that must stay cold, the animal that cannot be left behind, the ferry that may stop, the neighbor who needs a ride, the apartment with nowhere to store a garage-sized kit. When a household passage combines those realities, it is presented as a composite—not as testimony from a named survivor or evidence that the author personally lived the event.

Connected geography is not one culture. Cascadia.me does not borrow Indigenous motifs as regional decoration or imply authority it does not have. When Indigenous knowledge, cultural burning, or Nation-specific guidance belongs in the work, it should be attributed to the relevant people and source.

Cascadia.me carries no advertising or affiliate links. Reader support helps maintain the project, but no product recommendation earns a commission and no hazard is made louder to sell something. The guides, the workbook, and the Atlas are public and will stay that way. Nothing on this site moves behind a paywall.

Source notes

Four kinds of information appear here.

The label matters because each kind of information can support a different decision.

Observed
A current or recent report from a gauge, station, incident system, road source, or responsible agency. It is still bounded by reporting time and coverage.
Forecast
Modeled guidance for a future time. It is not an observation and cannot resolve every valley, ridge, shoreline, street, or household.
Historical or planning
Past records and generalized exposure context. These layers help a reader ask better questions; they do not predict the next event or determine parcel-level safety.
Official instruction
An order, warning, closure, or protective direction from the authority responsible for the place and event. This governs the immediate decision.
