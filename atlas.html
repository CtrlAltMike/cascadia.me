Read the map calmly.

This atlas is not here to create anxiety. It is a way to understand the landscape we already live in: faults, fire country, river valleys, and the places where preparation is practical.

Use it for orientation and planning. During an active event, follow your local emergency manager, fire district, weather service, or evacuation order.

How to read the Atlas data

Official feeds checked. Conditions can change faster than a map updates.

Coverage has gaps

Not every place publishes the same live data. An empty area can mean no report, no connected feed, or no mapped geometry—not necessarily no hazard.

Fire reports and perimeters can lag

Known “Out” fires are excluded during ingestion, but incident status, size, and mapped edges can still be delayed or incomplete. A fire point may appear before a perimeter does.

Wind layers have different meanings

Observed arrows are station reports, not a continuous wind field. Forecast shading is 10 km model guidance, not an observation or a prediction of fire or smoke movement. The model smooths mountain terrain; both layers can differ sharply from local wind across ridges, canyons, mountain passes, shorelines, and exposed slopes.

Planning layers are generalized

Historical fire and flood layers are simplified for regional viewing. They do not show parcel-level risk, current inundation depth, or a predicted fire path.

Official instructions come first

Use this map for orientation. During an event, follow evacuation orders and instructions from local emergency managers, fire agencies, and weather services.
