# The Resilience Network Constitution

## Purpose

NowWePlan.com, Cascadia.me, HurricaneCoast.me, and SanAndreas.me form a public-interest network devoted to one question:

**How can ordinary people make the lives they already have less brittle?**

The network exists to help people understand the places where they live, prepare for interruptions without organizing their lives around fear, recover more quickly when systems fail, and build relationships that make recovery possible.

We do not ask people to become survivalists. We do not sell catastrophe as an identity. We do not treat anxiety as evidence of seriousness.

We help people prepare calmly, incrementally, and together—then return their attention to living.

## The central idea

Resilience describes how a system behaves after a shock.

It is not invulnerability. It is not toughness. It is not the ability to prevent every loss.

A resilient household, neighborhood, building, or community experiences a shallower loss of function and returns more quickly. Every useful action serves one or both of those outcomes.

This gives the network its organizing principle:

> **Prepare for consequences, not for causes.**

Earthquakes, hurricanes, floods, wildfires, heat waves, winter storms, infrastructure failures, and other disruptions are physically different. Their domestic consequences overlap:

- Power fails.
- Water becomes unavailable or unsafe.
- Sanitation stops working.
- Indoor temperatures become dangerous.
- Air becomes unhealthy.
- Roads close.
- Communications become unreliable.
- Shops, schools, workplaces, and services become inaccessible.
- Households must remain in place—or leave immediately.
- Recovery lasts much longer than the event itself.

We organize preparation around these recurring consequences. Hazard-specific guidance is reserved for the places where specificity matters: the first minutes, the building, the route, and the local authority.

## The network promise

> **Understand what your place can do. Make the life you have less brittle. Know who you will face it with.**

Every part of the network must advance at least one part of this promise.

## The role of each domain

### NowWePlan.com: the method

NowWePlan owns the universal resilience framework.

It helps a household, group, or community turn awareness into an ongoing practice: identify essential functions, choose practical actions, assign responsibility, record agreements, review changing circumstances, and rehearse what matters.

It is not the authority on local hazards. It does not replace emergency managers, weather services, building professionals, public agencies, or local knowledge.

Its role is continuity: helping people keep a plan alive after the initial motivation fades.

The canonical essay “Resilience” belongs here because it explains why the entire network exists.

### Cascadia.me: the place as a connected system

Cascadia.me interprets resilience through the land, water, weather, infrastructure, and communities of the Cascadia bioregion.

It helps readers understand connected regional processes and translate them into local questions:

- What happens here?
- What interrupts ordinary life?
- What requires immediate action?
- How does a building like mine perform?
- Which official sources should I follow?
- What might recovery look like at my address?

Cascadia.me is the network’s first complete regional expression and the reference implementation for future regional sites.

### HurricaneCoast.me: the recurring season

HurricaneCoast.me interprets resilience across places shaped by tropical weather, storm surge, flooding, wind, heat, evacuation, insurance, repeated rebuilding, and long seasonal anticipation.

Its work extends beyond the arrival of a storm. It must address evacuation constraints, prolonged displacement, damaged housing, utility loss, financial recovery, unequal access to transportation, and the cumulative effects of repeated events.

It should help people inhabit hurricane country with knowledge and practical capacity—not spend every season in a state of dread.

### SanAndreas.me: the event without an appointment

SanAndreas.me interprets resilience across communities shaped by earthquakes and related ground failure, fire, infrastructure disruption, displacement, and recovery.

It must distinguish immediate protective action from the long domestic aftermath. It should make building performance, structural mitigation, water and sanitation, separated households, transportation disruption, and neighborhood response understandable without turning seismic risk into spectacle.

Its purpose is not to remind Californians continually that an earthquake is coming. Its purpose is to make the consequences more manageable whenever one does.

## The reader’s journey

Across the network, the reader should encounter a consistent progression.

### 1. Find the mark

Every place has a record of what it can do: a flood line, burn scar, damaged building, changed shoreline, relocated town, named storm, failed bridge, or year that remains in local memory.

We help readers find that record without implying that history is a countdown.

### 2. Know the first minutes

Immediate actions are hazard-specific. Readers should know the small number of actions that must be performed without research or deliberation when an event begins.

This guidance must come from appropriate official or expert sources and must remain visibly distinct from general preparedness advice.

### 3. Understand the building

The building is where regional risk becomes personal.

We help readers ask how a structure of their type, age, construction, and location is likely to perform—and what established mitigation can reduce the dominant failure mode.

We do not offer false precision, remote structural diagnoses, or generic assurances.

### 4. Prepare for consequences

The household should be able to preserve essential functions through common interruptions involving:

- Water
- Sanitation
- Safe temperature
- Clean air
- Light and information
- Food and medication
- Communication
- Money and documents
- Transportation
- Animals and dependents
- Staying in place
- Leaving quickly
- Reconnecting after separation

Preparation should be presented as increasing capability, not accumulating products.

### 5. Imagine month three

The event is not the whole disaster.

We ask what recovery could look like after the cameras leave: damaged housing, interrupted work, unavailable schools, unreliable utilities, insurance delays, scarce contractors, displacement, exhaustion, and the possible departure of neighbors and businesses.

This is not a prediction of ruin. It is a prompt to consider the resources, relationships, records, insurance decisions, and building improvements that shorten recovery.

### 6. Know who is nearby

A household is not an isolated unit.

Readers should be encouraged to establish specific, mutual, consent-based agreements with nearby people. Useful connection includes knowing who may need help, who can provide it, what skills and equipment already exist nearby, where the limits are, and how people will check on one another safely.

The network treats social connection as infrastructure.

## Editorial posture

### Begin with ordinary life

Our subject is not disaster. Our subject is the continuity of ordinary life.

We begin with homes, streets, schools, workplaces, animals, medications, meals, transportation, neighbors, weather, and the landscape people recognize.

The preservation and restoration of those things gives preparedness its meaning.

### Be serious without being alarmist

We describe consequential risks plainly. We do not minimize them, but we do not intensify them for attention.

We reject:

- Countdown language
- Imminence unsupported by evidence
- Catastrophe as entertainment
- Images selected primarily to frighten
- Tactical or militarized aesthetics
- Gear-centered measures of competence
- Shame directed at unprepared households
- Claims that official help will not come
- Claims that social order will collapse
- Treating uncertainty as certainty
- Treating preparation as a moral identity

Urgency must arise from the usefulness and timing of an action, not from emotional pressure.

### Favor agency over anxiety

Every serious statement should lead toward something the reader can understand, decide, ask, change, practice, or discuss.

When no meaningful individual action exists, we say so. We identify the institutional or collective intervention that would matter instead.

We do not transfer responsibility for unsafe buildings, fragile infrastructure, poverty, inaccessible transportation, or inadequate public systems onto individual households.

### Treat preparation as ordinary maintenance

Preparedness should resemble maintaining smoke alarms, renewing insurance, learning a route, storing familiar food, checking on a neighbor, or servicing a building.

It should not require adopting a new personality.

The desired emotional outcome is not vigilance. It is earned calm.

### Recognize unequal capacity

Preparedness is constrained by money, housing tenure, disability, health, transportation, work, immigration status, language, caregiving, and access to safe storage.

Advice written for a homeowner with a garage, vehicle, flexible job, and disposable income must not be presented as universal.

Where private capacity is limited, the community and public layers become more important—not less.

### Preserve the authority boundary

Official agencies issue alerts, evacuation orders, health guidance, weather forecasts, and emergency instructions. Qualified professionals evaluate buildings, engineering, medicine, insurance, and other specialized questions.

The network helps readers find, understand, and act on appropriate sources. It does not impersonate them.

When conditions are changing, current local instructions take priority over evergreen network content.

## The role of fiction

Fiction is the network’s deliberate exception to purely explanatory presentation.

The Field Stories may enter the lived experience of interruption more deeply than guides, maps, or workbooks can. They may show darkness, uncertainty, damaged routines, separation, discomfort, exhaustion, imperfect decisions, mutual aid, and the long work of returning.

Their purpose is not to frighten. Their purpose is to make resilience visible.

A successful story demonstrates:

- How ordinary capabilities become useful
- How plans meet imperfect circumstances
- How people improvise without becoming invulnerable heroes
- How neighbors alter outcomes
- How recovery unfolds through mundane acts
- How preparation helps without eliminating loss
- How care continues under pressure

Stories may contain danger and consequence because resilience cannot be demonstrated in a world where nothing goes wrong. But suffering must never become spectacle, and destruction must never be the principal attraction.

Every fictional work must be clearly labeled as fiction. Composite circumstances must not be presented as testimony, reporting, or personal experience. Material safety decisions must remain grounded in credible evidence, with factual notes kept separate from the story.

The stories should leave readers with recognition, compassion, and possibility—not dread.

## Public guidance and private planning

The regional sites provide complete, useful public guidance. They must never be weakened into advertisements for NowWePlan.

A reader who never uses NowWePlan should still be able to:

- Understand the relevant regional hazards
- Find official sources
- Identify immediate protective actions
- Ask informed questions about a building
- Build a practical household baseline
- Make a communication and movement plan
- Begin a neighborhood conversation

NowWePlan is an optional continuity layer. It helps people maintain what they have chosen to do: owners, next steps, review dates, reminders, agreements, and rehearsals.

No private household information should pass from a regional site to NowWePlan without the reader’s explicit, informed action.

Trust is more important than conversion.

## Shared structure, local expression

The regional sites share a doctrine, not a costume.

They may reuse underlying patterns, research methods, capability models, accessibility standards, and technical infrastructure. Each must still feel native to its place.

Its language, illustrations, history, landscape, infrastructure, seasonal rhythms, official sources, and practical constraints should arise from the region itself.

Cascadia is not California with different mountains. California is not hurricane country without rain. Hurricane country is not a generic coastline.

The common framework should make local difference more intelligible, not flatten it.

## Standards of evidence

Consequential claims must be traceable to credible sources.

The network should distinguish clearly among:

- Current official instructions
- Established safety guidance
- Scientific or engineering evidence
- Historical record
- Planning scenarios
- Model projections
- Reasoned interpretation
- Fiction

We state the limits of sources and models without using uncertainty to avoid useful conclusions.

We correct material errors openly.

## Measures of success

The network succeeds when a reader:

- Feels more capable and less afraid
- Understands one important fact about the place they live
- Knows the first action for a plausible local emergency
- Asks a better question about their building
- Improves one essential household capability
- Records one document or agreement
- Learns one neighbor’s name
- Makes preparation part of ordinary life
- Returns months later to review or practice
- Helps someone else recover

Page views, subscriptions, and conversions may support the work, but they are not its purpose.

The deepest measure is whether the network contributes to shallower drops and faster returns.

## The governing test

Before publishing any page, feature, story, alert, illustration, product, or campaign, ask:

1. Does this help someone understand their place or preserve ordinary life?
2. Does it clarify consequences or merely dramatize causes?
3. Does it increase agency without manufacturing fear?
4. Does it respect unequal circumstances?
5. Does it strengthen connection rather than isolation?
6. Is the authority and evidence boundary clear?
7. Does it help shorten the recovery—not only survive the event?
8. Will the reader be able to act and then set the subject down?

If the answer to those questions is no, the work does not belong in this network.

## Closing commitment

We believe serious risks can be held without obsession.

We believe preparation can be practical without becoming tactical, communal without becoming sentimental, and honest without becoming alarmist.

We believe the strongest household is not the one that imagines it needs nobody. It is the one that understands its dependencies, protects what it can, and participates in a living network of reciprocal care.

We will help people find the mark, understand the place, fix what can be fixed, prepare for recurring consequences, imagine the long recovery, and know the people nearby.

Then we will encourage them to go outside and live there.

The place was always worth preparing for.
