# The Resilience Network Voice Guide

**Status:** Canonical writing standard
**Version:** 1.1
**Date:** July 25, 2026
**Applies to:** NowWePlan.com, Cascadia.me, HurricaneCoast.me, and SanAndreas.me

This guide is subordinate to [The Resilience Network Constitution](resilience-network-constitution.md) and the [Resilience Network Brand Platform](resilience-network-brand-platform.md).

It applies to editorial pages, practical guidance, tools, navigation, interface copy, email, social copy, support messages, source notes, and campaigns. Current official instructions retain their original language when quoted.

## The governing voice test

> **Use a natural cadence. Would someone actually say this across a kitchen table?**

Use one companion test:

> **Would you say it this way to someone you care about who is already carrying a lot?**

The first test catches computed language. The second catches pressure, condescension, and easy assumptions.

## The voice

The network sounds:

- informed, but not institutional;
- warm, but not intimate on demand;
- candid, but not severe;
- encouraging, but not motivational;
- conversational, but not breezy;
- occasionally dry, but never flippant;
- specific to place and ordinary life;
- comfortable saying what's uncertain, expensive, inconvenient, or outside a household's control.

The voice doesn't perform calm. It earns calm by making the subject clearer.

## Spend emotional weight carefully

Serious subjects already carry weight. The copy doesn't need to keep adding more.

Describe what the reader can protect, continue, arrange, learn, or make easier. Use words such as *hard*, *loss*, *failure*, *fear*, and *danger* when they name an important fact, not as recurring atmosphere. One necessary warning can be direct. The next paragraph should usually return to a useful choice.

Positive language doesn't mean forced cheer or a promise that everything will be fine. It means keeping capability, continuity, and other people visible alongside the problem.

Use:

> Keep everyday life moving.

> A few records and contacts can make the next conversation easier.

Not:

> Make the next hard day easier.

> Recovery is a series of ordinary problems made harder.

## The relationship comes before the advice

Before telling readers what to do, notice what may make the action difficult.

This doesn't require an empathy preamble on every page. One accurate sentence is often enough:

> Storing water sounds simple until you picture the containers: where they'll fit, whether you can lift them, and how much they cost.

> Talking with a neighbor about emergencies can feel oddly intimate, especially if you usually exchange little more than a wave.

> Month three can bring a new round of ordinary tasks: finding a place to stay, reaching work, replacing medication, and keeping paperwork moving.

Recognition must be specific. Generic assurances such as “We know this can feel overwhelming” usually sound scripted.

## Natural cadence

### Use contractions

Contractions are the default in reader-facing prose:

- you are → you're
- do not → don't
- cannot → can't
- it is → it's
- that is → that's
- will not → won't
- you will → you'll
- we have → we've

Uncontracted forms may be useful for deliberate emphasis:

> An evacuation alert is not an evacuation order.

They may also remain in formal legal text, exact quotations, structured data, or labels where a contraction would reduce clarity.

Do not remove contractions merely to make writing sound authoritative.

### Vary sentence length

Conversation has rhythm. Let a longer explanatory sentence be followed by a short one when the thought calls for it.

> Photograph each room, including closets and cupboards, and keep the images somewhere you can reach if the device in your hand isn't the device you have later. It'll help with a claim.

Short sentences should carry weight, not create artificial drama.

Avoid stacks like:

> Water matters. Storage matters. Your choices matter. Start today.

### Stop when the line lands

Do not explain a clear statement, joke, image, or implication after it has done its work.

Use:

> Write down medication names, doses, and prescribers. “The small white one” is a poor description.

Not:

> Write down medication names, doses, and prescribers. “The small white one” is a poor description even on an ordinary Tuesday.

The added phrase weakens the line by explaining the joke.

### Read aloud

Read important copy aloud at normal speed.

Revise when:

- a sentence requires a second breath for no good reason;
- two phrases perform the same job;
- a contraction would sound more natural;
- the emphasis falls on an abstract noun;
- the sentence sounds like a campaign, report, grant application, or interface template;
- you would change the wording if a real person were sitting across from you.

## Sentence habits

### Prefer concrete language

Use:

> Keep enough water where you can reach and lift it.

Not:

> Increase household water resilience through accessible storage.

Use:

> Decide who'll pick up the children if you can't get across town.

Not:

> Establish a dependent reunification protocol.

### Let verbs do the work

Prefer:

- store
- carry
- call
- check
- ask
- leave
- return
- fix
- print
- share
- wait
- decide

Be suspicious of:

- facilitate
- empower
- operationalize
- leverage
- optimize
- activate
- navigate, when no one is navigating
- resilience, when a more ordinary word will do

### Use “you” carefully

Use second person for direct relevance and action. Do not use it to assign blame, presume resources, or narrate feelings.

Use:

> If you rent, the retrofit may not be yours to make. You can still ask when the building was constructed and whether it has been evaluated.

Avoid:

> You need to retrofit your home.

Avoid telling readers what they feel:

> You are probably overwhelmed.

Name the situation instead:

> The list gets long quickly.

### Make room for choice

Preparedness advice often contains real tradeoffs.

Useful phrases include:

- You may decide...
- If that isn't workable...
- Another option is...
- This won't fit every household.
- The right answer may depend on...
- There may be no meaningful household fix for this.

Do not soften immediate life-safety instructions that require direct action.

## Mild conversational humor

Humor may make awkward or unglamorous subjects easier to approach. Use it sparingly.

### The rule

> **The situation may be inconvenient, awkward, or faintly absurd. The reader is never the joke.**

### Good subjects

- containers that need somewhere to live;
- the number of mugs in a cupboard;
- paperwork;
- chargers that haven't been charged;
- bags that are too heavy;
- food nobody in the household likes;
- the awkwardness of starting a neighbor conversation;
- the fact that toilets require systems.

### Off-limits subjects

- active danger;
- evacuation or shelter instructions;
- injury, death, grief, or displacement;
- poverty, disability, illness, immigration status, or housing insecurity;
- a reader's lack of preparation;
- official warnings or uncertainty;
- institutional failure that has harmed people.

### Delivery

- Keep the useful information intact.
- Use understatement rather than a setup and punchline.
- Usually allow no more than one light line in a section.
- Do not call attention to the joke.
- Do not add a second phrase after the line lands.
- Remove humor that may not translate or that competes with clarity.

### Examples

**Water**

> Water takes up a surprising amount of space for something that normally arrives through a wall. Decide where it'll live before buying containers.

**Sanitation**

> This is the part everyone hopes another guide will cover. Unfortunately, the toilet won't become self-sufficient.

**Medication**

> Write down medication names, doses, and prescribers. “The small white one” is a poor description.

**Power**

> Check the battery pack occasionally. A backup at three percent is mostly a rectangle.

**Food**

> Store food your household already eats. An outage isn't a good time to introduce everyone to lentils.

**Neighbors**

> You don't need a committee or a clipboard. A name, a phone number, and a short conversation will do.

## Tone by context

The voice stays recognizable while the amount of warmth, explanation, and humor changes.

### Evergreen editorial

**Posture:** Observant, explanatory, companionable.
**Humor:** Available in small amounts.
**Cadence:** Natural paragraphs with varied sentence length.

> Most plans don't fail because nobody cared. They get buried under work, school, appointments, and the rest of the week.

### Practical household guidance

**Posture:** Specific, realistic, nonjudgmental.
**Humor:** Useful when it reduces awkwardness.
**Cadence:** Clear steps held inside enough explanation to support judgment.

> Start with the space you actually have. A few bottles in a cupboard won't cover a long outage, but they're more useful than an ideal supply you have nowhere to keep.

### Immediate action and live conditions

**Posture:** Direct, brief, calm, authoritative about the handoff rather than the event.
**Humor:** None.
**Cadence:** Short sentences and explicit verbs.

> If the ground is moving, Drop, Cover, and Hold On. If you're near the coast and the shaking is strong or lasts a long time, move inland or to high ground as soon as you can do so safely. Follow current local instructions.

### Recovery, rebuilding, and displacement

**Posture:** Patient, honest, restrained.
**Humor:** Usually none. Never use it near grief or material loss.
**Cadence:** Give difficult facts room. Do not rush toward uplift.

> Recovery can involve finding a place to stay, reaching work, replacing medication, documenting damage, and telling the same story to another office. You don't have to solve all of that in advance. A few records and contacts can make the next conversation easier.

### Buildings, health, insurance, and technical boundaries

**Posture:** Helpful without remote diagnosis.
**Humor:** Rare.
**Cadence:** State the limit, then help the reader ask a better question.

> A map can't tell you whether this building is safe. It can help you identify the ground conditions and the questions to take to a qualified professional.

### Interface copy

**Posture:** Plain and quiet.
**Humor:** Only in low-stakes, reversible moments, if at all.
**Cadence:** Familiar labels over branded language.

Use:

- Find a place
- Show official sources
- Print these pages
- Try again
- No recent report found

Avoid:

- Begin your journey
- Unlock your plan
- Take control now
- Stay one step ahead
- Resilience activated

## Openings

An opening should establish recognition, place, function, or a useful question. It should not announce the moral of the page.

### Begin with ordinary function

> Most days, the tap, toilet, refrigerator, phone, road, and card reader work without asking for attention.

### Begin with friction

> Storing two weeks of water is straightforward on paper and less straightforward in a small apartment.

### Begin with place

> A river can spend years inside its banks and still retain the rest of its floodplain.

### Begin with a question worth carrying

> If you couldn't return home tonight, who would know where to look for you?

Avoid universal campaign openings:

- You are stronger than you think.
- The next step starts now.
- Preparedness is power.
- Whatever comes, you have got this.
- It is time to take control.

They ask for emotional agreement before the page has earned it.

## Encouragement

Encouragement should follow recognition or action.

Use:

> A few bottles won't solve a long outage, but they're a useful beginning.

> If you wrote down the medication and the dose, that's one less thing to reconstruct under pressure.

> You don't need to finish the rest today.

Avoid:

> You have got this.

> Great job!

> Every step makes you stronger.

> You are now more resilient.

The network acknowledges progress without grading the reader.

## Calls to action

Write the task on the link or button.

Use:

- Find your local alert service
- Check whether your address is in the zone
- Print the medication page
- Choose a meeting place
- Read what this map can't show

Avoid:

- Learn more
- Get started
- Be ready
- Take action
- Keep exploring

Generic labels may be acceptable only when context makes the destination unmistakable.

## Endings

Do not end every page with inspiration. End when the thought is complete.

Useful endings may:

- state what's enough for now;
- offer one adjacent move;
- return to ordinary life;
- leave a question with the reader;
- stop after the practical conclusion.

Examples:

> If you know where the water will go and have filled the first container, you can leave the rest for another day.

> The next useful question is whether the people you live with could find the same information without your phone.

> Then put the paper somewhere you can find it.

Avoid:

> Together, we can build a more resilient tomorrow.

> Your journey has only just begun.

> The future is uncertain, but you are ready.

## Editing patterns to remove

### The empathy-performance opening

> We understand that preparing for emergencies can feel overwhelming.

Replace it with a specific observation:

> The list gets long quickly, especially when every item seems to require money or somewhere to store it.

### The slogan plus explanation

> Preparedness isn't about fear. It's about care.

If the surrounding writing already demonstrates care, the slogan is unnecessary. Name the actual subject.

### The command stack

> Make a plan. Build a kit. Protect your family. Start today.

Replace it with context and one proportionate action.

### The abstract benefit

> Build resilience and protect what matters most.

Replace it with the function:

> Keep medication cold during an outage.

### The overfinished joke

> “The small white one” is a poor description even on an ordinary Tuesday.

Stop sooner:

> “The small white one” is a poor description.

### The computed formality

> You don't need to complete every step today.

Use the natural cadence:

> You don't need to finish everything today.

## Review checklist

Read the page once for meaning, once aloud for cadence, and once as a reader with less control than the writer assumed.

### Relationship

- Does the page recognize before it advises?
- Does it sound adult to adult?
- Does it leave room for skepticism, ambivalence, and different choices?
- Does it avoid manufacturing intimacy or encouragement?
- Does it keep capability visible instead of repeating the emotional weight of the subject?

### Cadence

- Are contractions used where someone would naturally use them?
- Does sentence length vary for a reason?
- Can any sentence stop earlier?
- Has a joke, image, or conclusion been explained after it landed?
- Would these words be said across a kitchen table?

### Usefulness

- Is the first useful action proportionate?
- Does the reader know why it helps?
- Are alternatives available for money, space, tenure, mobility, health, transport, caregiving, and time?
- Is institutional responsibility visible where household action is not enough?

### Humor

- Is the situation the subject rather than the reader?
- Is the moment low stakes?
- Does the humor survive without a second explanatory phrase?
- Would removing it make the instruction clearer or kinder?

### Trust

- Are official instruction, established guidance, evidence, interpretation, and fiction distinct?
- Are uncertainty and source limits stated plainly?
- Does current local authority clearly take precedence?
- Can the reader act and then set the subject down?
