Download is only the beginning

Prepare a map for offline use.

Save it, test it, print it, and keep the changing facts separate. The map should still be usable when service and power are not.

  • AccountNot required
  • Location permissionNot requested

Offline readiness is a small chain of proof: the right publisher, the right map job, a known edition, a file that opens without a network, and a paper backup that can be read at the intended scale.

A map cannot make a route safe. Conditions, skill, equipment, weather, tides, closures, and judgment still matter. Turn around when the real place no longer matches the plan.

Eight steps

Make the map survive the trip.

Do this while you still have a dependable connection, a larger screen, time to print, and room to correct a mistake.

  1. Confirm the publisher and map job.

    Use the organization responsible for the question. A terrain map, trail map, legal vehicle-use map, and live condition page are not interchangeable.

  2. Record the title and edition.

    Write down the exact sheet, district, edition date, and the day you reviewed it. Keep the publisher page so you can check for a newer file.

  3. Save it on every device that needs it.

    Download the actual file. Do not rely on a browser tab, search result, thumbnail, or a link that still needs a connection.

  4. Test it in airplane mode.

    Turn off cellular and Wi-Fi. Reopen the file from the place you expect to find it, zoom in, move between pages, and confirm labels remain readable.

  5. Print a non-battery backup.

    Use the publisher’s print guidance. Avoid “fit to page” when scale matters, and verify any scale bar or known distance after printing.

  6. Protect and organize the map.

    Keep the paper dry and accessible. Give files plain names that include the place and edition so the correct map is obvious under stress.

  7. Record live checks and contacts.

    List the official condition, weather, tide, ferry, alert, permit, and land-manager sources that must be checked again close to departure.

  8. Practice locating and turning around.

    Find your starting point, identify prominent terrain, estimate distance, and agree on decision points. A planned turnaround is part of the route.

Airplane-mode practice

Practice accessing the map without a network

Practice the same steps you will use outside: unlock the device, find the file, orient the map, zoom without losing labels, and return to the trip overview. Ask another person to repeat the process without coaching.

Built-in device location may continue to work without cellular service, but do not assume every map file or reader can display it. Treat that capability as an aid, not the only way to locate yourself.

Test the failure, too.

  • Low battery or power-saving mode
  • A restarted or locked device
  • A wet screen or cold hands
  • The primary device being unavailable

Printing to scale

Check the paper, not only the setting.

If the map’s scale matters, follow its publisher’s instructions and print at actual size or 100 percent unless the map states otherwise. After printing, measure a scale bar or another known dimension.

Tile large maps across multiple pages only when you understand how overlap and scale are handled. Mark the map title, edition, adjoining sheets, emergency contacts, and the date of your final condition check.
