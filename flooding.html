Chapter three · Flooding

Flooding

A river begins rising long before water reaches a door. The household advantage is the time before a familiar road changes character.

River valleys, floodplains, steep drainages, and low ground across Washington, Oregon, and British Columbia · Flood clocks vary by place · Reviewed July 12, 2026

If water is rising or an evacuation instruction is active ↓

Immediate reference

If water is rising now.

  • Move now

    Flash Flood Warning, rapid rise, or evacuation instruction affecting your location

    Follow the notice. If a Flash Flood Warning affects a flood-prone location, move immediately to higher ground; do not wait for a route to be named. A B.C. Evacuation Order means leave immediately; U.S. local terminology varies.

  • Stop at water

    A flooded route is closed

    Do not walk, drive, swim, move a barricade, or try to judge the depth. Opaque water can conceal current, debris, electricity, or a missing road.

For slower river flooding: read the complete notice and the relevant local forecast point. A written household threshold can help you move earlier; it never replaces an official warning, order, or instruction. Do utility work only from a dry, safe location and according to the responsible utility or emergency authority.

A basin remembering water

Every river valley keeps a memory of where water can go.

The memory is visible in broad flat fields, side channels, wetlands, old oxbows, raised roadbeds, and the way a town gathers on the slightly higher ground. A floodplain is not empty land waiting for a river to misbehave. It is part of the river system, occupied between events by farms, homes, schools, warehouses, roads, and ordinary life.

Water begins gathering above the place where it is eventually noticed. Pacific storms meet mountains. Soil becomes saturated. Snow absorbs rain, melts, or stores it. Tributaries rise on different clocks. Culverts and storm drains take what they can. On steep ground or in a small drainage, the transition from rain to dangerous water may be much shorter.

Some river floods provide hours or days in which forecasts can be watched and a household threshold used. Flash floods can develop in minutes, including where rain is falling somewhere else. The useful plan never assumes the river will always be generous with time.

A gauge is one point, not a property-depth clock

Stage and flow describe conditions at a station. They may not translate directly to water depth, arrival time, drainage, or road access at a home. Observations may be provisional, and forecasts change as rainfall and watershed conditions change.

The route as a household threshold

The road can still look ordinary when it is time to leave.

Composite household: This is not a report of one flood or family. It brings together common river-valley conditions, household constraints, and established preparedness guidance.

Asha and Eli share a rented single-story house with their friend Tomas near a rain-fed tributary west of the Cascades. The river is below the road, out of sight behind cottonwoods. Tomas works at the local library and uses a cane outdoors. Their cat travels in a carrier. The only easy route begins on a low county road, then climbs.

They began paying attention to the road after a neighbor described which culvert backs up first. That local memory was useful, but it was not enough for a plan. They subscribed to the county alert system, saved the forecast point used by local officials, and wrote down the category at which they would move the car and leave. They chose a friend on higher ground who could receive the cat and provide an accessible room.

On a long wet night, the rain changes from background sound to something everyone is listening to. The ditch outside fills. Their official forecast point rises into the category they chose. No water is over the road, and no one is knocking on the door. This is the moment the rehearsal was for.

Asha puts current medication, chargers, documents, dry clothing, and the cane in the car. The cat goes into her carrier. The waterproof bins already hold the photographs and school papers they decided mattered most. Eli calls the uphill friend and then the newer household next door, not to predict what the river will do, but to make sure they have seen the same official notice.

They leave at their written household threshold, while the road is dry. That threshold can only move them earlier; it never replaces an evacuation instruction or other official direction. It is not the right threshold for every house or every river. They chose it because Tomas, the cat, one vehicle, and a low first mile mean waiting narrows their options.

From higher ground, they can keep reading the official notices without debating whether the road is still passable. They have not saved the house from a flood. They have protected the people, the animal, and the mobility they will need for whatever comes next.

A local threshold turns forecast time into usable time.

Storage, release, concentration, and spread

A watershed brings many small changes to the same channel.

Rain on a street and snow in a mountain basin are connected by slope, soil, tributaries, channels, wetlands, drainage, and time. Flooding begins before the water reaches the place where people notice it.

Catch and store

Mountains, soil, snow, wetlands, and reservoirs hold water.

How full, saturated, frozen, or warm those stores already are changes what the next storm or thaw can release.

Release and concentrate

Tributaries arrive on different clocks.

Heavy rain, snowmelt, urban drainage, and steep terrain can deliver water at very different speeds.

Spread

High water occupies low ground.

When a channel or drainage system can no longer carry the flow, water spreads through floodplains, side channels, and low routes. Each event draws a different edge.

Reading the water

The signal matters only when it belongs to your place.

Regional language is useful, but it cannot replace the local notice, forecast point, road authority, or evacuation instruction that connects water to a decision.

In the United States, a Flood Watch means flooding is possible; a Flood Warning means flooding is imminent or occurring; and a Flash Flood Warning calls for immediate protective action. In British Columbia, the River Forecast Centre uses High Streamflow Advisory, Flood Watch, and Flood Warning for increasing river concern. Evacuation terminology and authority still belong to the affected local jurisdiction.

A U.S. “100-year flood” is more clearly described as a 1%-annual-chance flood. It is a probability, not a once-per-century appointment. Flooding can also occur outside a mapped Special Flood Hazard Area, and an old map does not settle what a changed channel, drainage system, or larger event will do.

The Atlas cannot close those gaps. Its flood layer combines U.S. planning areas and generalized river-corridor cues. It does not show live depth, parcel certainty, every Canadian hazard area, or the route a household should take. The useful handoff is to the official source for the basin and road in front of you.

When the clock changes

A flash-flood warning, rapid rise, or evacuation instruction overrides bins, sandbags, photographs, furniture, and utility work. Leave by the current safe route.

Before the route changes

The plan belongs to the household, not the property.

Buildings matter, but people, health, animals, transportation, and a dry route come first. Property work is useful only while it remains safe and does not consume the time needed to move.

Begin by identifying the flood type. River flooding and flash flooding do not share one signal or timeline, and even nearby basins can respond differently. Save the responsible local authority, alert system, forecast point, and road source. Choose two routes when the geography allows it, and a destination that works for mobility, medication, caregiving, children, pets, service animals, or livestock.

Renters can move documents, medication, electronics, and sentimental objects higher; photograph possessions; ask the landlord how utilities and re-entry will be handled; and learn what renter’s insurance does and does not cover. Owners can ask qualified local professionals about raising equipment, backflow protection, drainage, flood openings, fuel systems, electrical work, and the limits of barriers or sandbags. Nobody should enter water to reach a control.

Insurance and assistance differ across the border. U.S. NFIP coverage, private insurance, Canadian overland-flood products, waiting periods, exclusions, and temporary housing are not interchangeable. Read the actual policy before the wet season and keep the insurer, landlord, lender, and recovery contacts somewhere the household can reach away from home.

Re-entry requires a new kind of patience. Wait until the responsible authority permits return, then consider the building itself: structural movement, electricity, gas or fuel, sewage, well water, contaminated food, mold, and damaged heating or ventilation equipment. Enter in daylight when possible. Never stand in water to operate electricity. Fuel-powered pumps and generators stay outdoors at least 20 ft / 6 m from doors, windows, and vents, with exhaust pointed away from buildings.

  • Which water can reach the places where this household sleeps, works, stores vehicles, or uses a road?
  • Which official notice, forecast category, or route condition triggers a move before water arrives?
  • Can everyone, including animals and mobility equipment, reach a safe destination while a planned route remains open and dry?
  • Which mitigation and utility steps are qualified, specific, and safe—and which are abandoned when time shortens?
  • Who receives the first message, and which neighbor or caregiving check-in is already agreed?
  • Where are the insurance, landlord, documentation, cleanup, health, and temporary-housing contacts kept?

After the water

The water can be gone while the work is only beginning.

Mud keeps the shape of a footprint. A waterline records a height nobody wanted measured. The familiar room may smell like river, fuel, sewage, or wet wallboard. Recovery asks for photographs, protective equipment, professional help, dry temporary space, and decisions about what can safely be cleaned or saved.

It also asks for memory. Which road went first? Which notice arrived in time? Which new neighbor had never seen the valley fill? A household plan cannot keep water in the channel. It can carry the people and knowledge of the place forward without asking anyone to prove their readiness by risking a life.

Keep this part close

Flooding quick reference.

Use the local notice, forecast point, and road authority. A generalized regional map cannot tell you that a route or building is safe.

Water rising now

  • If a Flash Flood Warning affects your flood-prone location, follow it and move immediately to higher ground; do not wait for a route to be named.
  • A rapid rise or evacuation instruction overrides household thresholds and property work.
  • Never walk, drive, or swim through floodwater or bypass a barricade.
  • Leave before a familiar road changes; property work can wait.
  • Operate utilities only from a dry, safe location under local guidance.

Before the wet season

  • Save the alert system, forecast point, road source, and local authority.
  • Write an earlier household threshold and identify every usable route; official instructions always take precedence.
  • Move documents and essential items higher; arrange qualified mitigation.
  • Read the actual insurance policy and keep contacts away from home.

Returning home

  • Return when the responsible authority permits re-entry.
  • Stay out if structure, electricity, gas, fuel, sewage, well, or septic safety is uncertain.
  • Document damage before major cleanup when it is safe.
  • Dry and clean as soon as safely possible under current health guidance.

Sources and limits

The source for your basin and road comes first.

This page does not claim to show a live regional river level, rainfall total, or complete alert picture. The Atlas does not show live inundation, parcel certainty, or complete British Columbia flood mapping.

During an event, use the alert, forecast point, road authority, band office, municipality, regional district, Tribal or local emergency manager, and evacuation instruction for the exact location.
