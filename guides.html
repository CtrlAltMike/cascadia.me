The Cascadia guidebook

Four guides.
Four different clocks.

Each hazard keeps its own clock. The landscape and the households living across it remain one continuous story.

Land shapes water. Weather moves through both. Fire follows fuel, terrain, and wind. Political boundaries decide who issues an instruction; they do not separate the physical system.

The chapters

Begin with the chapter closest to your place.

You do not need to read these in order. Begin wherever the land, season, or household question feels most familiar.

Each chapter begins in recognizable regional life, explains what the available signals can and cannot tell you, and follows the story into the household decisions that matter.

  1. I

    Ground, coast, and the first hours

    Earthquake

    Earthquake begins indoors: a table, a stairwell, a masonry wall, the shoes that can still be reached after the floor stops moving. It follows shaking outward to soft ground, bridges, ferry routes, and the coast, then into water, communication, medical needs, and reunion. Read it for the first movement and the longer hours that follow.

  2. II

    Dry land, wind, smoke, and routes

    Wildfire

    Wildfire begins at the dry edge of the year, long before every hillside looks dangerous. Road time is shaped by fuel, wind, smoke, animals, mobility, and how many ways a community can leave. The guide separates fire weather, observed fire edges, and evacuation instructions so a household can use the margin it has.

  3. III

    Rain, rivers, low roads, and coast

    Flooding

    Flooding begins upstream and often out of sight. A broad river may offer hours in which to watch a gauge; a steep drainage can change in minutes. This chapter connects forecasts and official notices to the low road, the uphill destination, and the household threshold that remains useful without pretending to predict water at the doorstep.

  4. IV

    Cold, power, access, and neighbors

    Winter Storm

    Winter Storm is not one kind of weather. The same Pacific system can bring rain to a shoreline, snow to a pass, ice to a valley, damaging wind to an island, and a long outage to a house on a well. Its practical clocks are warmth, water, medicine, power, roads, ferries, and the neighbors who help ordinary life continue.

The guide is one part of the work.

When conditions are changing, the Regional Hazard Atlas brings connected reports, observations, forecasts, and planning layers into one view. It supports orientation; local authorities remain the source for instructions.

When you are ready to turn reading into household work, Build Your Kit helps you think through water, safe warmth, communication, medicine, mobility, animals, and the people your plan must actually serve.
