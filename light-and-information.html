Keep life going · Light and information

Keep One Light and One Trusted Source Working.

Darkness makes ordinary work harder. Rumors do something similar. A useful setup gives you safe light, current official information, and enough power to check again later.

Begin with the path you walk at night.

Put a flashlight or headlamp beside each sleeping area and the main exit. Choose controls that can be used with the hands, vision, and mobility available in the household.

Identify one local alert source, one weather source, one utility source, and a paper way to find them. Verified information should not depend on remembering which account had the blue logo.

Your first move

Charge one power bank and test one flashlight tonight. Put the correct cable and spare batteries with them.

Use power where it changes a decision.

Light the task

Prioritize exits, stairs, medication, sanitation, cooking, caregiving, and repairs. Headlamps keep hands free; lanterns can light a room without open flame.

Choose information sources

Enroll in local alerts and save official emergency-management, weather, utility, road, transit, school, and building contacts. Record the jurisdiction each one covers.

Build charging layers

Use wall charging first, then tested power banks, vehicle adapters, solar or other sources that fit the household. Reserve power for essential communication and devices.

Keep a radio option

A battery or hand-crank NOAA Weather Radio can provide warnings when internet or cellular service is unavailable. Learn the controls before the room is dark.

Make information perceivable and usable.

Access needs

Plan vibration, visual alerts, captions, volume, screen readers, large print, language, relay, and backup batteries for hearing or communication devices.

Fire and carbon monoxide

Keep smoke and CO alarms working with battery backup. Do not trade safe lighting for candles around children, animals, oxygen, or unstable surfaces.

Shared buildings

Know how management communicates when elevators, doors, alarms, water, or power fail—and what happens if the app cannot be reached.

Longer interruptions

Set check-in times instead of refreshing continuously. Rotate devices, preserve batteries, and use public notice points or trusted messengers when networks stay down.

Do not use a household post, group chat, or map as an official alert. Time-stamp information, name the issuing authority, and correct old reports visibly.

One person can monitor; everyone can keep living.

Agree who checks which official source and when the group will update. This reduces duplicate battery use and the strange human urge to refresh bad news every twelve seconds.

CDC’s power-source guidance covers lighting, chargers, batteries, medical equipment, alarms, radios, and safe generator planning. CDC backup power guidance.
