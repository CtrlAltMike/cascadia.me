Chapter I

The warning came two days early and sounded like weather.

Casey Ostrom read it Saturday morning on her phone in the barn aisle, coffee in one hand, while the sprinkler ticked across the arena and twenty horses worked through their hay: a Red Flag Warning for the Cascade west slopes, Monday afternoon through Tuesday, east winds 25 to 40 with gusts to 60, relative humidity in the single digits. The Weather Service had used a phrase she'd never seen them use, set off in its own line like they wanted you to trip on it — a potentially historic wind event over critically dry fuels — and Casey stood there a moment in the cool of the aisle, in the smell of fir and shavings and horse, and felt the words try to mean something and fail, the way a bank balance fails to mean anything until the card declines.

It had been the driest summer anyone could document. Ninety-one days since real rain, the Cispus — the river that drains all that high Goat Rocks country down to the Cowlitz — running at a September skeleton of itself, the big valley firs carrying their needles like paper, and everyone had spent August saying the sentence everyone in the valley said in August — boy, one spark — the way you say things you've decided not to think about. But the drought wasn't the news. The wind was the news. Fire weather on the wet side of the mountains was a marriage of two things, and the dry half showed up most Septembers now and mostly nothing happened, because the wet side's winds came off the ocean, cool and obedient. The east wind was different. The east wind came downhill from the desert side, falling and drying and accelerating like something poured, and it happened maybe a handful of Septembers in a generation, and when it happened over fuels like this, it was the mechanism behind every old fire the Northwest told stories about instead of thinking about.

Casey knew this the way she knew most things: as a chore list. She spent the weekend doing what she always did with a bad forecast, which was work. Topped off the stock tanks while the well pump still had grid power to run it. Checked the tires and the wiring on both trailers, greased the gooseneck ball, backed each rig up to the loading chute and left them aimed out. Mowed the strip along the driveway she always mowed. Charged everything in the tack room that took a charge. It was not, she'd have been the first to say, a plan. It was the horse world's ambient religion — a boarding stable is twenty different families' irreplaceable animals standing in wooden buildings full of dry hay, and you do not run one for eleven years, as Casey had, without fire living somewhere in your spine. There was a laminated sheet on the tack room wall titled EVACUATION that she had written herself after the smoke summer of '20, and made every boarder read at signing, and updated twice, and never once used. Her twelve-year-old, Milo, called it "mom's doom sheet." Her father called it nothing, because her father did not acknowledge it.

Her father was the fourth generation on the place and had opinions about fire the way other men had blood types. Erv Ostrom was seventy-two, a timber faller for forty years, built like the stump of something significant, and he had grown up six miles up Camas Creek Road on the stories of his own grandfather, who in September of 1902 — a boy of nine, on a homestead near Yacolt — had stood in a creek for a night and a day with wet sacking over his head while the largest fire in Washington's history went over the top of him and took the world. Half a million acres in thirty-six hours, on an east wind, on the wet side. Ash fell on ships at sea. In Seattle they lit the streetlamps at noon. Erv had heard it at the kitchen table his whole childhood, and the strange chemistry of family stories had worked on it the way it works: three generations of retelling had converted the warning into folklore, into proof of the blood's toughness rather than the fire's reach, and what Erv Ostrom had ultimately extracted from the biggest east-wind fire in state history was the lesson that an Ostrom with water and nerve comes out the other side.

He'd fought fire, too — decades of it, the working kind, pickup crews and dozer lines in the seventies and eighties — and it had taught him a thousand true things about ordinary fire that were all, though nobody knew it yet that weekend, about to become dangerously false. Fires laid down at night: true his whole life. Fires backed downhill slowly: true his whole life. A green mowed field, a metal roof, a good pond and a gas pump bought you a defendable place: true, mostly, his whole life. Erv's fifty years of habits — the mowing he did like liturgy, the pastures grazed to lawn, the woodpile away from the house, the pond he'd dug in '86 and the Honda pump in the shed beside it — added up to what a state fire pamphlet would have called exemplary defensible space, and what Erv called keeping the place up, and underneath the chores lay a bedrock conviction Casey had never once dislodged in forty-one years as his daughter: that leaving was what you did with children and animals, and staying was what you did with land.

Sunday evening the utility called. A robot voice, apologetic in advance: due to forecast extreme fire weather, power may be shut off to your area beginning midday Monday, September 7, and remain off for the duration of the event. Casey stood on the porch listening to it with the phone on speaker and the evening going gold and utterly still — the stillness before an east wind has a texture, a held-breath quality, the alders not moving at all — and across the yard in the doorway of his shop her father listened too, wiping his hands on a rag.

"Shutting off the power," he said. "So their line doesn't start the fire they're scared of. And every well pump in this valley goes dead the same hour, so if a fire does start, nobody's got water to fight it with but me." He shook his head slowly at the whole century. "You want to know the difference between your generation and mine, that's it right there. You people manage risk. We kept pumps."

"Dad."

"I know." He raised the rag, peace. "I saw your trailers pointed out. That's good. You'll take the horses and the boy if it comes to something." It was as close as he ever came to blessing the doom sheet. He looked off up the valley, where the light was leaving the ridges in the long way it did in September, and Casey followed his eyes, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing up there yet. Just timber, sixty years of it since the last cut, standing in the still gold air drier than it had ever been in either of their lifetimes, waiting on the wind like everything else.

Monday came in blue and wrong. The wind arrived at eleven, out of the northeast, warm as a hand dryer — and that was the detail Casey would put first every time she told it after, the warmth, wind that should have been cool coming down off the mountains arriving instead like the opening of an oven door, humidity dropping through the twenties, the teens, the barn thermometer's little dial swinging as if it were being interrogated. The power went out at 12:15 with a district-wide clunk. And at 1:50 in the afternoon, fourteen miles up the valley, somewhere in the drainage of Camas Creek's east fork — cause never determined, the investigation would eventually say, though the valley had its theories and kept them — something that had been waiting all summer got what it was waiting for.

Casey was in the arena teaching nothing, fixing a gate latch, when Milo came out of the house holding the battery radio and said, in a voice that had not yet decided how scared to be:

"Mom. They're saying there's a start up the east fork. They're saying it's moving."

Chapter II

Here is what Casey Ostrom did with the first two hours, and she made herself account for it afterward, minute by minute, not as penance but as engineering, because the two hours were where the whole thing was decided and she wanted to know exactly what had almost decided it wrong.

She looked at the smoke first. Everyone did. It stood up over the east-fork country like a gray tree, leaning hard west-southwest, already taller than any smoke she'd seen from the place in eleven years — and her first coherent thought was a distance: fourteen miles. Fourteen miles was the far side of the world. Fourteen miles of drainages and ridges and the whole green machinery of the wet side that had eaten every fire of her lifetime down to an inconvenience. She had watched columns before. In '20 they'd sat on the porch and watched one over the shoulder of the ridge for six days while it wandered around a burn scar eating lichen, and the sum of its menace had been three weeks of bad air and a canceled horse show.

So she did reasonable things, at a reasonable pace, and the reasonable pace was the trap. She called the boarders — got nine of fourteen, cell service already sagging as the valley's one tower went over to its backup batteries — and told them what she knew, which was a location and a wind. She told them she'd started prepping in case. She said in case eleven times in ninety minutes; she counted, later, in her mind's replay, each one a little payment of tribute to the world where this was still Tuesday. She brought the four school horses in off the far pasture. She hooked the gooseneck to the dually — reasonable — and then spent twenty minutes she did not have looking for the second truck's hitch pin — unreasonable, unforgivable, the pin was in a coffee can exactly where it lived, but her hands had started doing that thing hands do, orbiting tasks instead of landing on them — and at 3:40 the county's alert system pushed a Level 1 for the upper valley, Camas Creek Road above milepost 8, READY: BE AWARE OF DANGER IN YOUR AREA, MONITOR OFFICIAL CHANNELS, and Casey stood in the driveway reading it and performed the calculation that every person in every fire valley performs on that alert, the calculation the levels are built to invite: Level 1. Milepost 8. We're at milepost 6. It's not even our level yet.

What broke the spell — and she gave the credit forever after, publicly, at the community meeting in October with the boy sitting mortified beside her — was Milo, who had spent the two hours where twelve-year-olds spend a crisis, on the radio and the porch with binoculars, and who came to her at ten past four with the scanner in his hand and a piece of paper where he'd been writing things down, because he was her son.

"Mom. The fire crossed the east fork at three." He held out the paper like homework. "The guy on there said it's making runs. He said —" checking his own handwriting — "'sustained runs of three to four miles an hour with spotting a mile ahead.' " He looked up at her. "It went four miles since lunch. I did it on the calculator. If it's four miles an hour and it's ten miles away —"

Casey stood very still in her own driveway while her son's arithmetic arrived, the way arithmetic had been arriving on people across the region all afternoon, and she felt the two worlds swap weight — felt fourteen-miles-is-the-far-side-of-the-world become a number with a denominator, felt every fire she had ever personally watched reveal itself in one cold second as a different species from the thing up the valley, a housecat portfolio backing a claim about tigers. The wind gusted hard enough right then to bang the arena gate against its post, warm as a dryer vent, and out of fifty years of her father's stories the one true detail rose up through the folklore and stood in front of her: a nine-year-old boy in a creek, and the part her great-great-grandmother had always insisted on, the part the family told as color and had never once told as data — it came on them in the night, faster than a man could run, and it had been fourteen miles off at supper.

Fourteen at supper.

"Get your go-bag and Grandpa's radio," Casey said, and her voice had changed, and Milo heard it change and went. And Casey Ostrom walked into the tack room, took the laminated sheet off the wall, and started making the calls that ended the word in case.

The plan on the sheet was built on a piece of horse-country doctrine she had written at the top in marker and made every boarder initial: WITH ANIMALS, LEVEL 1 IS LEVEL 3. It had a logic she'd stolen from a wildfire clinic in Ellensburg years ago and never needed: an evacuation's cost curve runs backward for livestock. A family in a car can leave in four minutes at the last siren. Twenty horses need three hours, two drivers, multiple round trips, and daylight, and every one of those inputs is only available early — which means the price of moving horses at Level 1 is a wasted afternoon and some ribbing at the feed store, and the price of moving them at Level 3 is that you don't. You choose between looking foolish and being empty-handed. The sheet knew all this. The sheet had known it for four years. The gap — and this was the engineering finding of her minute-by-minute accounting, the thing she'd teach afterward to anyone who'd listen — the gap was never the plan. The gap was the permission. The sheet couldn't grant it. Some human being on the ground has to be the first one to act like the emergency is real, out loud, in front of neighbors, while the sky is still mostly blue overhead, and it had taken her two hours and a twelve-year-old's long division to become that person.

At 4:25 she called the fairgrounds in Chehalis, sixty miles west, where the extension office had stood up a large-animal intake that morning on the strength of the same forecast she'd read in the barn aisle Saturday — somebody down there had needed no long division at all — and a woman with a clipboard voice said, "How many and how soon," and Casey said, "Twenty, and the first load leaves in forty minutes," and saying it made it true, which is the other thing she'd teach: that in the early hours of a disaster, sentences do not describe your plans. They create them.

Then she called her father, whose landline still worked, the old copper trunk humming along indifferent to the dead grid, and told him the east fork had crossed and the fire was making runs and she was pulling the horses now, all of them, and there was room in the plan for one more old man.

Erv was quiet a second. Behind his silence she could hear his kitchen radio, the same scanner traffic Milo had, so he knew everything she knew, and had run it through fifty Septembers of a different arithmetic.

"Wind's supposed to blow through tomorrow night," he said. "It'll lay down after dark some. They always do." A pause, and then, gentler, the closest thing he had to a concession: "You're right to move the horses. That part was always right. You run your sheet, Case. I'll run my pump."

"Dad —"

"I've got fifty years of mowing between me and anything that comes down that valley, a pond, and no mortgage on any of it." The old rag-wiping calm, infuriating, granite. "Somebody has to be here to put out what lands on the roof. That's how places make it. That's how this place made it in '02 —" and there it was, the folklore doing its two-generation damage in real time — "and it'll make it now. Go on. You've got loading to do and the light won't wait."

She had twenty horses and a son, and she did not have the two hours it would take to lose this argument the way she'd lost it her whole life. That was the true shape of the choice, and she made it the way everyone in the valley was making their versions of it that afternoon, fast and forever: she said, "Keep the landline close. I love you. I'm sending nobody up this road after dark, so don't you dare need it," and her father said, "Never have yet," and Casey hung up the phone and went to load horses under a sky that was, by then, no longer mostly blue.

Chapter III

The load-out ran two hours and eleven minutes, and pieces of it stayed with her the way the dish towel stays with some people and the doorway with others — bright, specific, permanent.

The convergence, first. She had called nine boarders; what arrived over the next half hour was seventeen vehicles, because the horse world has a phone tree older than phones, and it does not send sympathy, it sends trucks. Paige Okafor came with her three-horse slant and her teenage nephew before Casey had finished the second call. A man Casey knew only as Roy with the mules appeared with a stock trailer still dusty from an elk camp and said, "Point me," and was pointed. Two women from the barrel-racing barn down toward Randle, total strangers to half these horses, walked into the aisle pulling on gloves and started wrapping legs with the brisk unsentimental gentleness of people who had done this before in some other valley, some other September. Nobody organized it. It ran on the sheet, on aisle order, on the ancient discipline of barn people in a crisis, which is that you may cry in the truck but not in front of the horses.

Because the horses knew. That was the second thing that stayed. Horses read the world through their skin, and the world that afternoon was wrong in every register a prey animal owns — the light going amber, the wind hot and gusting to forty, the air beginning, faintly, to smell — and animals who had loaded a thousand times planted and blew and rolled their eyes white at the trailer's dark mouth. Casey's old lesson mare walked on like a professional and stood like a rock, bless her taxpaying heart, and steadied two others by example. The young ones came apart. It took four people and twenty minutes to load a five-year-old gelding worth more than Casey's truck, and at the end of it the barrel racer holding the tail rope said, matter-of-fact, wiping her forehead with her sleeve, "That's the trade nobody puts on the pretty checklist. Every hour you wait, the loading gets slower. The fire speeds up and the horses slow down. It's a scissors."

Level 2 for the whole road above milepost 4 came through at 5:50 — SET: BE READY TO LEAVE AT A MOMENT'S NOTICE — and it changed the light in people's faces more than the sky had. The first two rigs were already gone west by then, Paige hauling four, Roy hauling five, sixty-mile turnarounds into a sun that was no longer exactly a sun but an orange thing you could look at directly, hung in smoke that had gone from a tree to a wall to a weather of its own, with the column's head — Milo, on the porch with the binoculars, narrated it in the flat awed voice of a boy discovering meteorology at the worst possible seminar — building a cloud on top of itself, a real cloud, white and cauliflowered, miles high: the fire making its own sky now.

And at 6:30, with eleven horses out and nine still standing in the aisle, Casey did the arithmetic she had known since Ellensburg she might someday have to do, and it did not come out even, and she took the can of livestock marker off the shelf below the laminated sheet, where it had waited four years like a court summons, and paint-marked her phone number in green on the shoulders and rumps of the four she could not fit or could not load — the two half-broke colts who had said their final word on trailers, the retired draft cross too arthritic for a fast haul, and Biscuit, the lesson pony, twenty-six years old, master professor of nine hundred children, who could have loaded blindfolded but for whom there was, at the end of the arithmetic, no square foot left — and she led them out to the big west pasture, the one with the pond corner and the grazed-short grass and no barbed wire, and cut the far fence wide open with bolt cutters so nothing could trap them against a line, and slipped their halters, and let them go.

The colts ran. The old draft dropped his head and grazed, unbothered, a monument. Biscuit followed Casey back along the fence line on the inside as far as the corner, the way he had followed ten thousand children at the end of ten thousand lessons, expecting the routine to resume, because his entire long faithful life had taught him that the routine resumes; and Casey Ostrom stood at the corner post in the orange wind with green paint on her hands and told a twenty-six-year-old pony that she was sorry, and that he was to stay by the water, and then she turned around and did not look back, because the last two loads were waiting, and the discipline is that you may cry in the truck.

She went through the house in nine minutes with a duffel and the fireproof document box, and the nine minutes taught her a final thing about herself that she'd spend the fall repairing: she was magnificent on animals and paperwork and empty on her own life. Coggins certificates, ownership papers, insurance, passports, the box — all reflex, thirty seconds. And then she stood in the hallway of the house she'd grown up in, with the light coming through the windows the color of the inside of a jack-o'-lantern, and could not think of a single personal thing, drew an actual blank, her mother's cedar jewelry box six feet away on the dresser and invisible to her, forty-one years of photographs on the stairwell wall and her eyes sliding off them — some fuse in her blown clean through by two hours of triage — and it was Milo, again, Milo who ran back in against orders and came out with his own school backpack in one hand and, in the other, the stairwell photo of Casey's mother on the gray mare at the '89 county fair, saying, "You always look at this one when you walk by. I watch you do it."

Twelve years old. She put it face-down on the dashboard so she wouldn't cry before the driving was done, and at 7:05 p.m., with the last four horses loaded and the hay barn doors rolled shut out of some helpless housekeeping instinct, three rigs pulled out of Ostrom Stables onto Camas Creek Road, headlights on at seven in the evening under a sky the color of a bruise, into a line of vehicles coming down off the upper road that was not yet a flight and would be one within the hour.

Behind them, six miles up, her father stood in his mowed field in the ember-smelling wind with a garden hose in one hand and a Honda pump primed by the pond, one man and fifty years of chores against the twentieth century's largest bet, and the last thing Casey saw of the place in her mirrors was his yard light — off, of course, dead with the grid — and the shop window, where a kerosene lamp had come on, small and steady and older than every mistake being made in the valley that night.

Chapter IV

The road did the last of the teaching.

Camas Creek Road ran six miles down to Randle and the highway, two lanes of patched county asphalt with the creek on one side and timbered slope on the other, and at 7:05 it carried maybe thirty vehicles, spaced, orderly, headlights in the false dusk — early people, sheet people, horse people. By 7:40, when Casey came back through on the second-to-last leg of the relay, it carried a hundred, and the spacing was gone. And at 8:10, the interval collapsed entirely, because at 8:10 the system did the thing the after-action reports would spend a year explaining: the Level 3 for the entire road — GO NOW: LEAVE IMMEDIATELY — went out to a valley that half couldn't receive it. The cell tower's backup batteries had died at 7:52. The alert landed on every phone with a signal, and there was no signal. It reached the people already watching official channels on battery radios, which is to say the people already gone or going, and it missed, almost surgically, the exact population it existed for: the waiters, the it's-not-our-level-yet households, the people whose plan was the alert.

What reached them instead was the nineteenth century. Two sheriff's deputies and a Forest Service rig driving the road with sirens and PA — LEVEL THREE, LEAVE NOW, DO NOT PACK, LEAVE NOW — and volunteer firefighters pounding on doors, and neighbors laying on horns in driveways, the whole ancient analog technology of the human alarm, working downhill house by house in the dark ahead of a light that had begun, unmistakably now, to show over the eastern ridgeline: not flames, not yet, but a pulsing orange presence under the smoke, like a city where no city was.

Casey drove the last load out at 8:35 with Milo counting rigs behind them in the mirror, and the pinch came at the milepost 2 narrows, where the road bent hard along the creek under a steep timbered face, and a spot fire — an ember, one ember, flown two miles ahead of everything on the wind — had taken hold in the brush above the guardrail and run up the slope like a struck match, so that the last quarter mile before the flats ran between the creek on the left and a hillside on the right that was actively, casually catching, fire moving through the understory with a sound she heard even over the diesel, a sound like rain falling upward. Sparks crossed the road in horizontal streams. A trooper stood at the narrows in the ember-fall waving traffic through with a flashlight, methodical as a school crossing, shouting the same three words at every window — keep moving, don't stop — and the horses rode it out in the dark of the trailer with their heads low, and Milo held the photo face-down on his knees with both hands, and it was thirty seconds, maybe forty, and then it was pasture and the lights of Randle and the highway, and behind them the narrows stayed open another hour and a half, which was enough, barely, for almost everyone.

Almost. The Teagues came down at eleven, Ross and Marion, from their place a mile below Erv's — Ross Teague who had stood in his yard at sunset with a garden hose telling his wife the same true old sentences, it'll lay down after dark, fires back downhill slow, we've got the creek — and who learned at 10:30, in the space of about four minutes, what everyone east of the mountains had learned in 2020 and the valley learned that night: that the old rules were written by ordinary fires, and this was no longer an ordinary fire, and an east-wind run does not lay down after dark, because it is not being driven by the sun. It is being driven by the whole atmosphere of the interior draining toward the sea, and the interior does not sleep. The fire came over the eastern ridge above the Teague place at 10:40 not as a line but as a front, crowning, throwing fire ahead of itself, with a sound Ross would only ever describe one way — a jet that never passes — and the Teagues left their dinner on the table and their doctrine in the yard and drove out through the narrows with the paint blistering on the tailgate, and made it, both of them, whole.

Ross's brother Wade did not. Wade Teague, fifty-five, had left with the first wave at seven, early and easy, his truck loaded, everything right — and at nine, from the safety of the highway, in the long anxious idle of the evacuated, he had done the thing the fire courses beg people not to do and the heart does anyway: he had gone back. For his two dogs, which in the load-out's chaos had bolted from the truck and not come, and which he could not sit in Randle and leave. He told his brother he'd be forty minutes. The deputies at the narrows had already fallen back by then; there was no one left to stop him, only the sign, and no sign ever stopped a man whose dogs were on the wrong side of it.

They found his truck in his own driveway, doors open. That was as much as the valley ever let itself say in detail, and it is as much as needs saying here. The county's fire killed one person, and the finding that mattered, the one Casey would carry into every clinic and meeting for the rest of her life, was this: it did not catch him fleeing. It caught him returning. The deadliest hour of an evacuation is not the leaving. It is the second trip — the trip made by people who did everything right the first time, from the terrible safety of hindsight's edge, backward across the line for the one thing love went and counted after the counting was closed.

Casey heard about Wade two days later, at the fairgrounds, and stood for a while at the panels with her forehead against the old lesson mare's neck, doing the math she couldn't stop doing: that at seven o'clock, standing at the pasture corner with green paint on her hands, some version of her had already been offered the same trade, and had walked to the truck instead, and that the difference between her and Wade Teague was not virtue. It was that her list had made the decision back in daylight, in cold blood, years ago, and his had been left to make itself at night.

Chapter V

The fairgrounds were their own country, sixty miles west, and the country was called Waiting.

The large-animal barns had filled through the night with the region's ark — three hundred horses by dawn, plus goats, llamas, a disgruntled emu, pens of 4-H rabbits stacked in a stock trailer — and around them a second encampment of humans, living in trucks and borrowed campers in the gravel lots, showing each other their phones' last loaded maps. The extension office ran intake off clipboards. Church vans arrived with chili. A whiteboard by the announcer's booth grew a column of taped index cards headed ANIMALS SEEN LOOSE — descriptions, road names, phone numbers — that people stood and read like scripture, and Casey added a card for four head, green paint, west pasture, and could not make her handwriting behave.

The sky did the talking. All night the eastern horizon pulsed under the smoke, sixty miles of pulse, and Tuesday's sun rose the color of a signal flare into a sky that stayed the deep amber of a photograph of some other planet, and ash fell on the fairgrounds all day, fine as talc, on the trucks and the panels and the backs of the horses, ash that had been the east-fork country, falling politely on Chehalis. Milo caught some on his sleeve and looked at it a long time and didn't say what he was thinking, and Casey didn't ask, because she was thinking it too: some of this was somebody's trees, and some of it, statistically, was somebody's everything.

The not-knowing about Erv lasted forty hours, and it had a structure, and the structure was the copper phone line. The landline had rung through Monday night — she'd called at 9:40 from the fairgrounds lot and gotten him, wind roaring in the line's background like surf, his voice almost cheerful with adrenaline and argument: pump running, roof wet, spot fires in the field and him putting them out as they landed, "like whack-a-mole, except the moles are on fire." Then, at 11:10, mid-sentence — he was saying something about the pond level — the line went to a silence she knew instantly wasn't a hang-up, the particular dead-wire silence of infrastructure meeting flame somewhere in six miles of burning right-of-way, and after that there was nothing. No line, no tower, no road, no overflights in wind like that. Forty hours of a silence with her father inside it somewhere, alive or not, and no instrument on earth tuned to the difference.

She ran the fairgrounds the way she ran everything, because idle hands would have eaten her alive — hauled water, worked intake, walked a hysterical woman through the loose-animal board, taught two teenagers to wrap legs — and she learned the thing every fire family learns in the Waiting country, which is that the mind negotiates. It builds the pond in loving detail, the mowed field, the metal roof, fifty years of chores, and rests its whole weight on them; then at 3 a.m. it builds the other version with the same materials and the wind at sixty; and there is no evidence for either, and morning comes anyway, and you haul water.

Wednesday afternoon a Forest Service liaison with a bullhorn and a printout read the first verified damage assessments off Camas Creek Road to a crowd of two hundred standing in the ash-fine gravel, address by address, in the flattest voice mercy could manufacture, and the crowd learned its fates in alphabetical order, cries and exhalations scattered through it like weather; and Ostrom, 6180 Camas Creek — structures standing, resident accounted for went through Casey Ostrom's body like current through a wire, and she sat down on a hay bale in front of everyone with her son's arms around her neck, and let the truck rule lapse at last, there being no horses in view.

Chapter VI

They let residents back up the road on Saturday, escorted, in convoys, and the valley they drove into was a lesson printed at landscape scale.

The word for it was mosaic, the fire scientists' word, and from inside it the word meant: incomprehensible mercy and incomprehensible loss, interleaved at the scale of a coin flip. Green timber standing untouched beside slopes burned to standing charcoal. A hayfield unburnt to its edges around the white ash footprint of the house it had belonged to. The Teague place: gone to the foundation, the doctrine's yard a gray page, and beside it the woodshed, untouched, cordwood stacked neat as the day Ross split it. Mailboxes stood at driveways that led back into nothing, and at other driveways, into everything, whole homesteads green and dumb and fine, and the first thing the mosaic burned away was any language of deserving. The valley never afterward said spared like it meant something moral. The fire had not read anyone's heart. It had read their gutters.

Because that was the pattern under the coin flips, and Casey saw it before the assessment teams wrote it up, driving the road at fifteen miles an hour with her heart in her teeth: the houses that stood, stood mostly for reasons. Metal roofs. Cleared eaves. Green mowed ground. No firewood against the wall, no bark mulch at the foundation, no arborvitae — the realtors' shrub, the little green gas cans — planted in a line beneath the windows. The fire that took the Teague house never touched it with a flame front; the front had passed a quarter mile north. It took the house the way the fire took most of the houses it took that night, with an ember in a gutter full of fir needles, twenty minutes of nobody there, and physics. The difference between the standing and the gone was rarely the fire's path. It was the previous ten years of Saturdays.

Which was the sermon of her father's place, when they came around the last bend and it rose up out of the black — and it did rise out of black, the fire had come fully down to it, the pasture fences charred to the wire, the west hillside a comb of burnt poles — the barn, the arena, the house, the shop, standing on their island of grazed-short green like an exhibit, like the state pamphlet's back-page photo, fifty years of mowing drawn on the land in the one ink the fire respected. And in the middle of it, in a lawn chair in the yard, in the same clothes, gray to the eyebrows with ash and sleeplessness, her father, who stood up when the truck came in and then had to put a hand on the chair, and Casey was out the door before the engine stopped.

He was three days recovering his voice enough to tell it whole, and he only ever told it whole once, that October, standing up at the community meeting in the Randle fire hall with his hands gripping the folding chair in front of him, because Casey had asked him to and because Wade Teague's brother was sitting in the second row. He told them about the first hours, the whack-a-mole hours, when the plan worked and the pump ran and a strong old man with fifty years of chores put out everything the wind delivered, and how right it had felt, how confirmed. And then he told them about 11:30, when the fire came down the west hill — downhill, at night, the two things fire never did in fifty years of his knowing — not as spots to whack but as a single breaking wave of light, and the wind ahead of it snatched the water off his hose sideways into vapor, and the sound arrived, the jet that never passes, and Erv Ostrom, seventy-two years old, fourth generation, understood in one wingbeat of that roaring dark that every true thing he knew had been true about a different animal, and that he was not defending the place, had never for one minute been defending the place, the place was defending him — fifty years of mowing buying him a hundred-yard moat he could not have bought that night with any act of courage at any price — and he did the last smart thing left on the shelf, the thing his grandfather's story had been trying to teach the family for a hundred and twenty years before the folklore sanded the terror off it: he walked into the pond, up to his chest, behind the low earth dam, with a wet wool blanket over his head, and he stood in the water in the middle of the fire while it went over and around his moat and took the west hill and the fences and the night, and he was not brave in the pond, he told the fire hall, his voice gone to gravel; he stood in that water and hollered for his own father like a boy, and nothing in fifty years of fires had told him it would be like that, because he had never met the animal, only its housecats.

"I lived," Erv told them — and this was the part the county extension woman asked, afterward, if she could quote in the pamphlet, and he said yes, put my name on it — "because of five decades of Saturdays, one pond, and luck the size of God. Two of those three you can't order in. So here's an old man's whole opinion on defending your place, and I earned it in the water: do your Saturdays. Do every one of them. And then when they call it, leave, and let the Saturdays do the defending. The work stays. Send the body out." He looked at the second row, at Ross Teague, and his hands were not steady on the chair back. "And whatever's still inside the line when the road closes — I don't care what it is, I don't care what it loves you back like — it's gone ahead of you or it's gone. There is no second trip. That's the whole meeting, far as I'm concerned. There is no second trip."

The horses came home through October, one phone call at a time, the green paint doing its work: the draft cross ambling up a driveway nine miles west four days in, singed and imperious; the colts together on a logging landing at two weeks, feral and fine, walked down by Roy and his mules like a parable. Biscuit they found in the first week, and Casey went herself, because you go yourself. He had done what she told him. He was in the pond corner of the west pasture — the low wet ground, the green margin, the oldest and wisest real estate on the place, chosen by twenty-six years of horse sense — and the fire had taken the west pasture around him, and the margin had very nearly been enough, and had not been. She sat in the burned grass beside the old pony for a long time, in the rain that had finally, uselessly, begun, and said the only thing there was to say, which is what she'd said at the corner post: that she was sorry, and that he had been the best of them, and that nine hundred children were going to be told he ran with the colts.

The colts, in fact, is what the children were told. The valley keeps a short list of its permitted lies, and that one made the list unanimously.

Chapter

Chapter VII

The rains closed the fire out in late October, the way the wet side always closes its arguments, gray and thorough, and the valley entered the long second disaster nobody films — the insurance winter, the trailer-on-the-foundation winter, the winter of chainsaws and hazard trees and FEMA paperwork and the particular silence of the Teague driveway — and came out the other side of it changed in the way Casey had by then stopped being surprised that places change: not hardened. Organized.

The clinic she taught in April, in the same fire hall, was her own curriculum: the doom sheet, revised, retitled, photocopied. It said WITH ANIMALS, LEVEL 1 IS LEVEL 3 at the top, same as always. But the revisions underneath were the winter's whole education, and she walked the room through them line by line. The plan is not the gap; permission is the gap — decide in daylight, years ahead, exactly what you will do at Level 1, and then do it while the sky is blue and the neighbors are watching, because someone has to be first and it might as well be you. The alert is a courtesy, not a covenant — the tower can die, the levels can leapfrog; own a battery radio and act on the weather, not the notification. Your Saturdays are your firefight — do the gutters, the eaves, the mowing, the ember math, and then leave, and let the work stand your post. And at the bottom, in bold, in a box, the line the whole county knew by then whose water it was carried in on:

There is no second trip.

Erv mows still, of course. More, if anything — he has annexed the road margins now, an old man on a tractor extending the moat a little every year, and the utility flags him as a nuisance and the fire district flags him as a resource and both are correct. But the pump sits in the shed with its gas drained, retired to legend, and on Red Flag days — there were two the following September; there will be more every September now, that is simply the century they've been issued — on Red Flag days Erv Ostrom tops off the stock tanks, checks the trailer tires without being asked, and puts his own bag, packed, by his own door, where his daughter can see it from the arena.

The photograph of Casey's mother on the gray mare went back on the stairwell wall, one nail hole to the left of where it had hung for thirty years. Nobody discussed the move. But it hangs now directly above the shelf where the truck keys live, at exactly the height of a passing glance, and Milo, who put it there, and who is thirteen now and does the evening feed and monitors three scanner channels with the sovereign authority of a boy who was right once, says the reasoning out loud only when asked, and says it the way his family says everything, which is as a chore:

"It's on the way out the door. Grab it going by. Zero seconds." A shrug. "Now it's on the list."
