Chapter five · Volcano

Volcano

Most days, the volcanoes of Cascadia are simply part of the horizon. Preparedness begins by learning whether water, wind, or neither connects your home to one of them.

Northern California, Oregon, Washington, and southern British Columbia · Local hazard zones and instructions come first · Reviewed July 15, 2026

If volcanic activity or ash is affecting your area now ↓

Immediate reference

If volcanic activity is affecting your area now.

  • Leave without delay

    Evacuation order or lahar warning

    Use the official route and instructions for your location. Move away from the hazard zone; do not enter a valley or closed area to watch, collect belongings, or take photographs.

  • Reduce exposure

    Ash is falling; no evacuation order

    Shelter as directed. Keep ash out of indoor air, avoid unnecessary travel, and protect breathing and eyes when you must be outside. Follow local health, water, and cleanup guidance.

  • Do not drive into it

    You are away from home or in transit

    Do not enter ash, a closure, or a restricted hazard area. Use official route, transit, aviation, air-quality, and emergency sources for the place you are in.

Location changes the correct action. A household in a mapped lahar valley may need to evacuate while a household farther downwind may need to shelter from ash. Follow your local government, Tribal authority, First Nation, emergency manager, or incident official.

The horizon and the watershed

The nearest summit is not the whole map.

Cascadia’s volcanoes are monitored, mapped, and usually quiet. The useful first question is not “How close am I?” It is “What path could a hazard take to reach this place?”

Fast, destructive hazards near an erupting volcano are only part of the picture. Lahars—flows of water, rock, and volcanic debris—can follow river valleys far beyond a mountain’s flanks. Ash can move with the wind across watersheds and borders. Road, aviation, power, water, and supply disruptions can reach households that are outside a mapped ground-hazard zone.

This is why a generic circle around a summit is not a household plan. Begin with an official volcano-specific hazard map. Notice the valleys, high ground, routes, schools, workplaces, and places where people in the household spend time. Then learn which agency will issue an evacuation instruction and which scientific source will describe changes at the volcano.

A scientific notice is not a local evacuation order

USGS observatories describe volcanic activity and hazards. Local and Tribal emergency authorities decide protective actions for communities. In B.C., follow the local government or First Nation and EmergencyInfoBC. Keep the source, place, time, and instruction attached to every alert.

One update, two plans

The same mountain does not ask every household to do the same thing.

Composite households: Elena and Jules are fictional. Their routes, ash plan, work schedules, animals, and official sources combine ordinary regional circumstances with established guidance.

Elena lives near a broad river valley draining a Cascade volcano. The mountain is distant enough to disappear behind low cloud, but her address is inside a mapped lahar planning area. Her local plan does not point toward the summit. It points across the valley and uphill, along a route she has walked with her child.

Jules lives much farther away, east of the crest. His neighborhood is outside the volcano’s mapped ground-hazard zones. Wind, not a river valley, is the connection he prepares for. He keeps NIOSH-approved N95 respirators that fit the adults, protective goggles, animal supplies, medication, and the instructions for reducing outside-air entry into the home. He knows where the rainwater collection system can be disconnected.

When a scientific update appears, both households read it. Elena checks the local emergency source and keeps the route ready. Jules checks the affected area, wind and ash guidance, then listens for local health and travel instructions. Neither treats a social post, a dramatic image, or the color of an aviation notice as a household order.

Their preparations are different because the paths are different. Both are calm because the decision was made while the mountain was quiet.

Distance matters. The watershed and the wind explain what distance alone cannot.

The timing is different

Build gradually. Use a warning to act, not to shop.

Earthquakes begin without warning, and a wildfire can quickly narrow the time available to leave. Cascade volcanoes often show signs of unrest days to months before an eruption. That usually gives household supplies a longer runway, but it does not guarantee one.

  1. While conditions are quiet

    Map first. Add supplies slowly.

    Learn whether a lahar valley or likely ashfall connects the household to a volcano. Practice the local route now. Fold respirators, goggles, medication, animal needs, and other relevant items into the ordinary household kit as time and budget allow.

  2. If unrest increases

    Refresh the plan you already made.

    Follow the volcano observatory and the emergency authority for your location. Check routes, transportation, prescriptions, communication, and ordinary supplies. An increase in scientific attention is a reason to become attentive, not a household evacuation order by itself.

  3. If a local warning reaches you

    The preparation window has closed.

    Follow the instruction for your location. Leave immediately for an evacuation or lahar warning; shelter and reduce exposure when directed during ashfall. Do not delay protective action to gather more equipment.

One part of the plan cannot wait for unrest

Some lahars can occur with little warning, including from a landslide or slope collapse. If home, work, school, care, or recreation is inside a mapped lahar area, learn and practice the route during ordinary life. The supplies can be gradual; the route knowledge should already be there.

Water, air, and connected systems

Volcanic hazards travel along different paths.

One volcanic event can create very different conditions across the region. The path that reaches a place helps determine whether the plan begins with movement, shelter, or continuity.

Near the volcano

Some hazards leave very little room for improvisation.

Pyroclastic flows, falling rock, landslides, lava, gases, and other near-volcano hazards require official closures, exclusion areas, and evacuation instructions to be taken literally.

Downstream

A lahar route is a local map and a practiced movement.

Learn whether home, school, work, or recreation lies in a mapped zone, which route reaches high ground, and what natural or official warnings apply locally.

Downwind

Ash preparation protects air, water, equipment, and time.

Respiratory protection, goggles, a cleaner indoor space, medication, animal needs, water protection, limited driving, and careful cleanup can matter far from a volcano.

Read the issuer before the color

Volcano status, aviation color, and local action are three different things.

A single screenshot can remove the place and purpose that make an alert useful. Keep each system attached to the agency that issued it.

For U.S. volcanoes, USGS uses ground-based Volcano Alert Levels—NORMAL, ADVISORY, WATCH, and WARNING—and a separate Aviation Color Code—GREEN, YELLOW, ORANGE, and RED. The ground level describes overall volcanic activity and ground hazards. The aviation color communicates conditions relevant to aircraft and ash in the atmosphere. Neither system, by itself, tells a household which road to take.

Local governments, counties, Tribal authorities, First Nations, parks, emergency managers, and incident officials issue closures, evacuations, shelter instructions, and return information. Their affected area may be much smaller than a regional scientific notice, or may extend through a valley beyond what looks close on a small map.

Source What it answers What it does not answer alone
Volcano observatory What scientists are observing at a volcano and how the activity is classified. Whether your household must evacuate, shelter, or use a particular local route.
Local or Tribal emergency authority What people in a defined place should do now, including routes, closures, evacuation, shelter, and return. A complete scientific explanation of the volcano or conditions outside its jurisdiction.
Weather, air quality, road, water, or utility agency How ash, visibility, travel, drinking water, power, or other systems are being managed. The full volcanic picture or every local protective action.

The Atlas shows reference locations, not hazard zones

Cascadia.me’s Atlas can help locate major regional volcanoes and hand you to official mapping. A point marks a summit or volcanic center. It does not show the extent of lahars, ashfall, closures, or current volcanic activity.

Build the part of the plan that belongs to your path.

A household near a lahar valley needs a practiced local movement. A household that may receive ash needs protection for breathing, indoor air, water, animals, and equipment. Many households need both.

Map before equipment. Open the official hazard map for each place the household spends time. If any location is in a mapped evacuation or lahar planning area, record the official route and destination, then practice it under ordinary conditions. Include school, work, visitors, animals, mobility, transportation, and the possibility that people are separated.

Prepare for ash conditionally. Add properly selected respiratory protection, protective goggles, medications, animal supplies, and a plan for reducing ash entry into indoor air. Know how local guidance may affect ventilation, rainwater collection, drinking water, vehicles, machinery, electronics, and cleanup. People with respiratory or cardiovascular conditions should follow their clinician’s advice.

Keep the information chain short. Save the volcano observatory or national science source, the local emergency authority, and the relevant road, air-quality, water, and health sources. In the U.S., the free USGS Volcano Notification Service can deliver scientific notices by email. It does not replace local emergency alerts.

Plan for the longer interruption. Ash and closures can interrupt travel, school, work, deliveries, aviation, power, and water even when a household is outside a direct ground-hazard zone. The ordinary household capabilities—communication, medicine, mobility, water, food, power, caregiving, and reconnection—still do most of the work.

  • Are home, work, school, care, or recreation locations inside an official volcano or lahar planning area?
  • Which official route reaches the destination, and can everyone use it with the time, mobility, animals, and transportation available?
  • Where are the respirators, goggles, medication, animal supplies, and cleaner-air space for ashfall?
  • How will the household protect drinking water or disconnect rainwater collection if officials advise it?
  • Which science, emergency, health, road, air-quality, water, and utility sources apply to this place?
  • Where will separated household members go, and who can help if roads, schools, transit, or communication change?

A mountain can remain part of the horizon without becoming a source of constant alarm.

Preparedness does not require rehearsing catastrophe every time cloud gathers over a summit. It asks for a few durable facts: whether a mapped valley reaches your life, where the local route goes, how the household would keep ash outside its lungs and water, and which officials will speak for the place.

Learn those things while the volcano is quiet. Then the mountain can return to what it is on most days: weathered rock, snow, forest, water, and a familiar direction in the landscape.

Keep this part close

Volcano quick reference.

Follow the local or Tribal emergency authority for protective action. Use official volcano science, ash, health, road, water, and utility sources for the conditions they describe.

Evacuation or lahar warning

  • Leave immediately by the official route and follow closures.
  • Move away from the mapped hazard area; do not approach the volcano or valley.
  • Take people, mobility needs, essential medication, animals, and the go bag if they are ready.
  • Do not delay to gather nonessential belongings or observe the event.

Ashfall without evacuation

  • Shelter and manage ventilation as local officials direct.
  • Avoid unnecessary travel; ash reduces visibility and can damage vehicles.
  • Use a NIOSH-approved N95 respirator and goggles when exposure cannot be avoided.
  • Protect water, animals, medication, electronics, and indoor air using current guidance.

Cleanup and return

  • Return or begin cleanup only when officials say it is safe.
  • Avoid dry sweeping that puts ash back into the air.
  • Follow local disposal, water, roof, HVAC, vehicle, and equipment guidance.
  • Ash can be heavy and slippery; use qualified help for unsafe roofs or systems.

Sources and limits

The official map and authority for your place come first.

Volcano-specific hazard zones, routes, local alerts, access restrictions, water systems, and emergency procedures vary. A regional guide cannot determine a household’s protective action during an event.

The Atlas uses reference points and official handoffs; it does not reproduce hazard zones or current volcano status. Signals helps locate official publishers but is not a live alert feed.
